# Exercise 5.25: When the most probable symbol gets one bit

Source: Cover and Thomas, *Elements of Information Theory*, 2nd ed. (2006), printed pp. 148–149. Logarithms are base 2 unless explicitly written as ln.

## Solution

Assume at least two symbols and the stated strict inequality between the largest and second-largest original masses.

## (a)
Suppose the symbol of mass $`p_1>2/5`$ is merged before the final root merge. At that moment, at least three nodes remain. Since it is among the two smallest selected nodes, all but possibly one other node have mass at least $`p_1`$. Four or more remaining nodes would therefore have total mass at least $`3p_1>1`$, impossible. Thus exactly three remain, with masses $`p_1,a,b`$, where the algorithm merges $`p_1`$ with $`a`$ and $`b\ge p_1`$. Since $`a=1-p_1-b<1/5`$, we have $`a<p_1\le b`$.

The node of mass $`b`$ cannot be an original symbol, since $`p_1`$ was strictly largest. It was formed from two child nodes earlier. New Huffman merge masses are nondecreasing: after merging the two least masses $`u\le v`$, each next available mass is at least $`v`$, so the next merge sum is at least $`2v\ge u+v`$. Since $`a<b`$, node $`a`$ must already have existed when $`b`$ was formed; otherwise its later formation would violate this monotonicity. Both children merged to form $`b`$ were no greater than $`a`$, so $`b\le2a`$. Consequently
```math
1=p_1+a+b\ge p_1+\frac b2+b\ge\frac52p_1>1,
```
a contradiction. Thus $`p_1`$ survives to the final merge and gets length 1.

## (b)
If $`p_1`$ has length 1, it is one child of the root; the other child's mass is $`1-p_1`$. Immediately before that other child was formed, its two constituent nodes were merged while $`p_1`$ remained available. Each must therefore have mass at most $`p_1`$. Hence $`1-p_1\le2p_1`$, or $`p_1\ge1/3`$. Contraposition proves that $`p_1<1/3`$ forces length at least 2. If only two symbols existed, $`p_1\ge1/2`$ anyway.
