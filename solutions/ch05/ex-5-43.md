# Exercise 5.43: A marker followed by binary digits

Source: Cover and Thomas, *Elements of Information Theory*, 2nd ed. (2006), printed pp. 157. Logarithms are base 2 unless explicitly written as ln.

## Solution

The requirement as written is unique decodability, and the initial marker alphabet $`\{A,B,C\}`$ is disjoint from the following bit alphabet $`\{0,1\}`$. This boundary information matters.

## Literal uniquely decodable optimum
In source order, choose
```math
(A,\ B,\ C,\ A0,\ A1,\ B0).
```
Every occurrence of A, B, or C marks a new codeword. Splitting at those markers (and at the message endpoint) recovers the words uniquely, even though, for example, A is a prefix of A0. The lengths are $`(1,1,1,2,2,2)`$, so
```math
L=\frac{16+15+12+2(10+8+8)}{69}=\frac{95}{69}\approx1.3768115942.
```
This is optimal: there are only three possible length-1 codewords, and nonsingularity is necessary for unique decoding. The three largest masses must receive those shortest words by the exchange argument; every remaining word has length at least 2. The construction attains this lower bound.

## If an instantaneous tree is additionally required
With three branches at the root and binary branches afterward, Huffman-style merging of the smallest pairs until three nodes remain gives $`8+8=16`$, $`10+12=22`$, and $`15+16=31`$. Putting these three nodes under the root yields, for example, $`(C1,C0,B1,B0,A0,A1)`$. Every length is 2, and the minimum prefix-tree mean is 2. The sibling-exchange argument proving binary Huffman optimality applies below the root, so the pair-merging rule is optimal under this stronger condition. It differs from the literal uniquely decodable problem because that problem can use the marker characters as explicit boundaries.
