# Exercise 11.6 — Bias and mean-square error of variance estimates

Source: Cover and Thomas, *Elements of Information Theory*, 2nd ed. (2006), printed pp. 401.

## Solution

Assume $`n\ge2`$.

### (a)
Linearity gives $`\mathbb E\bar X_n=n^{-1}\sum_i\mathbb EX_i=\mu`$, so the sample mean is unbiased.

### (b)
The identity $`\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2=\sum_i(X_i-\mu)^2-n(\bar X-\mu)^2`$ has expectation $`n\sigma^2-\sigma^2=(n-1)\sigma^2`$. Consequently $`\mathbb ES_n^2=(n-1)\sigma^2/n`$ and $`\mathbb ES_{n-1}^2=\sigma^2`$.

### (c)
For Gaussian observations, $`U=\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}`$, so $`\mathrm{Var}U=2(n-1)`$. Hence
```math
\mathrm{MSE}(S_n^2)=\frac{2(n-1)+1}{n^2}\sigma^4
=\frac{2n-1}{n^2}\sigma^4,
\qquad
\mathrm{MSE}(S_{n-1}^2)=\frac2{n-1}\sigma^4.
```
Their difference, unbiased minus biased, is $`(3n-1)\sigma^4/[n^2(n-1)]>0`$ when $`\sigma^2>0`$. Thus the biased estimator has smaller mean-square error. The chi-square fact follows by orthogonally decomposing the standardized Gaussian sample into its mean direction and its $`n-1`$ orthogonal residual coordinates.
