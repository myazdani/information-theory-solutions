# Exercise 16.10: A larger-mean independent stock merits positive weight

Source: Cover and Thomas, *Elements of Information Theory*, 2nd ed. (2006), printed p. 655.

## Solution

In the usual well-defined growth-optimization setting, **yes**: the all-second-stock allocation cannot be optimal. Assume first that $`X_2>0`$ almost surely, $`0<EX_2<EX_1`$, and the baseline expected log is finite. Independence and Jensen's inequality give

```math
E\frac{X_1}{X_2}=EX_1\,E\frac1{X_2}
\ge\frac{EX_1}{EX_2}>1,
```

with an infinite left side allowed. The relative return on moving fraction $`t`$ into stock 1 is $`1+t(R-1)`$, where $`R=X_1/X_2`$. Define
$`g(t)=E\ln(1+t(R-1))`$. For $`0<t\le1/2`$, $`g(t)/t`$ is bounded below at the integrand level by -2, and Fatou's lemma gives

```math
\liminf_{t\downarrow0}\frac{g(t)}t\ge E(R-1)>0.
```

Consequently some sufficiently small positive allocation improves expected log growth. Thus any genuine optimal portfolio must give stock 1 positive weight. This does not say that its entire weight should be in stock 1; mean return alone does not determine the optimal fraction.

A qualification is needed for the literal formulation allowing arbitrary nonnegative variables and extended log growth. For example, let independent $`X_1`$ take 0 and 4 with equal probability, and $`X_2`$ take 0 and 2 with equal probability. Then $`EX_1=2>1=EX_2`$, but both vanish simultaneously with probability $`1/4`$. Every portfolio has expected log growth $`-\infty`$, so every allocation is a vacuous raw maximizer, including the all-second-stock allocation. Without a nonruin/well-defined-growth assumption, the literal unconditional claim is therefore false. Under the chapter's meaningful log-optimal model, the derivative argument above is the intended conclusion; normalized relative growth can handle many integrability problems, but cannot create wealth in a state where all assets are zero.
