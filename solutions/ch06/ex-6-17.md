# Exercise 6.17 — St. Petersburg growth with and without cash

Source: Cover and Thomas, *Elements of Information Theory*, 2nd ed. (2006), printed pp. 181–182.

## Solution

The exercise has two separate lists labeled (a), (b); they are distinguished below. Logarithms measuring growth are base 2.

### First (a)
For $`\Pr(X=2^k)=2^{-k}`$,
```math
\mathbb EX=\sum_{k\ge1}2^{-k}2^k=\infty.
```
### First (b)
Write $`K=\log_2 X`$. Then $`\mathbb EK=\sum k2^{-k}=2`$. With all wealth invested, the strong law gives
```math
\frac1n\log_2 S_n\longrightarrow2-\log_2 c.
```
Thus $`S_n\to\infty`$ for $`c<4`$ and $`S_n\to0`$ for $`c>4`$, almost surely. The zero-growth fee is $`c^*=4`$; the strong law by itself does not assert convergence of wealth at equality.

### Second (a)
For the invested fraction $`b`$, differentiate the concave objective on $`b>0`$:
```math
(\ln2)\,W_b(b,c)=\mathbb E\frac{X-c}{c(1-b)+bX}.
```
At $`b=1`$ this is $`1-c\mathbb E(1/X)=1-c/3`$. Strict concavity shows $`b^*=1`$ for $`c\le3`$, and $`0<b^*<1`$ for $`c>3`$. The derivative at zero is positive infinite, so all cash is never optimal at a finite fee.

### Second (b)
Here is a convergent one-dimensional characterization, also useful for computation. For $`t\ge0`$ define
```math
A(t)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{2^{-k}}{t+2^k},\qquad
c(t)=\frac1{A(t)}-t,\qquad b(t)=1-tA(t).
```
The first-order equation with $`t=c(1-b)/b`$ gives precisely these formulas. At $`t=0`$, $`c=3,b=1`$. Moreover
```math
c'(t)=\frac{\mathbb E[(t+X)^{-2}]}{A(t)^2}-1>0,\qquad
b'(t)=-\mathbb E\frac{X}{(t+X)^2}\lt 0.
```
The first strict inequality is the positive variance of $`(t+X)^{-1}`$. Thus $`b^*(c)`$ decreases continuously from 1 to 0 after $`c=3`$. The parameter $`c(t)`$ tends to infinity, as also follows from the asymptotics below.

### Second (c)
The exact rate is $`2-\log_2 c`$ for $`c\le3`$. For larger fees, insert the unique $`t`$ above into
```math
W^*(c)=\sum_{k\ge1}2^{-k}\log_2(t+2^k)-\log_2(t+c).
```
It is positive for every finite $`c`$: the positive infinite derivative at $`b=0`$ implies some small positive bet improves on zero growth. The envelope theorem and the first-order equation give
```math
\frac{dW^*(c)}{dc}=-\frac{b^*(c)}{c\ln2}\lt 0.
```
More quantitatively, set $`B(t)=1-tA(t)=\sum_{k\ge1}(t+2^k)^{-1}`$. Splitting the sum at $`\lfloor\log_2t\rfloor`$ gives $`tB(t)=\log_2t+O(1)`$; the error is uniformly bounded by geometric-series estimates on either side of the split. Since $`c=tB/(1-B)`$, we obtain
```math
c=\log_2t+O(1),\qquad
b^*(c)=\Theta(c2^{-c}),\qquad W^*(c)=\Theta(2^{-c})\quad(c\to\infty).
```
For the final statement, $`0\le W^*(c)\le\mathbb E\log_2(1+X/c)\to0`$ by dominated convergence, and integrate the derivative estimate from $`c`$ to infinity. The exact parametric expression is preferable to asserting a constant asymptotic prefactor, because the dyadic payoff lattice can produce bounded oscillations.
