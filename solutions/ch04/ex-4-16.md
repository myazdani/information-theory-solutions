# Exercise 4.16: A run-length-limited binary source

Source: Cover and Thomas, *Elements of Information Theory*, 2nd ed. (2006), printed pp. 94–96. Logarithms are base 2 unless explicitly written as ln.

## Solution

## (a)
Interpret state 1 as a most recent symbol 1, state 2 as one zero since that 1, and state 3 as two zeros since it. State 1 must go to state 2 by emitting 0. State 2 can return to state 1 by emitting 1 or move to state 3 by emitting 0. State 3 must return to state 1 by emitting 1. Thus between successive ones there are exactly one or two zeros; the forbidden patterns are 11 and 000. Conversely a permitted word tracks these states uniquely after its initial symbol, with initial 1 in state 1 and initial 0 in state 2, as specified by the initial vector. These are precisely the arrows in the source figure. Endpoint conventions for finite prefixes do not affect exponential growth.

## (b)
A path ending in state 1 must have come from 2 or 3; a path ending in 2 must have come from 1; a path ending in 3 must have come from 2. Therefore
```math
X_1(n)=X_2(n-1)+X_3(n-1),\quad X_2(n)=X_1(n-1),\quad X_3(n)=X_2(n-1),
```
which is the stated matrix recurrence. The one-symbol words 1 and 0 supply $`X(1)=(1,1,0)^T`$.

## (c)
For distinct eigenvalues, let $`E_i`$ denote the spectral projector for $`\lambda_i`$. Then $`A^{n-1}=\sum_i\lambda_i^{n-1}E_i`$, so the requested fixed vectors are $`Y_i=E_iX(1)`$. The characteristic polynomial is
```math
\det(\lambda I-A)=\lambda^3-\lambda-1.
```
The graph is strongly connected and has cycles of lengths 2 and 3. Thus $`A`$ is primitive; its positive Perron eigenvalue strictly dominates the other eigenvalues in modulus. Its positive left and right eigenvectors show that the Perron coefficient of the nonzero nonnegative initial vector is positive in every coordinate. Consequently $`X_i(n)=c_i\lambda^{n-1}(1+o(1))`$ with $`c_i>0`$, proving the logarithmic limit. The positive root is
```math
\lambda=\sqrt[3]{\frac12+\sqrt{23/108}}+\sqrt[3]{\frac12-\sqrt{23/108}}
\approx1.3247179572,
\qquad\log_2\lambda\approx0.4056852314.
```

## (d)
The stationarity equations are $`\mu_2=\mu_1`$ and $`\mu_3=(1-\alpha)\mu_2`$. Normalizing gives
```math
\mu=\frac{(1,1,1-\alpha)}{3-\alpha}.
```
The first-coordinate equation $`\mu_1=\alpha\mu_2+\mu_3`$ then also holds.

## (e)
Only state 2 has a random next state, so $`h(\alpha)=h_2(\alpha)/(3-\alpha)`$. The derivative has the sign of
```math
(3-\alpha)h_2'(\alpha)+h_2(\alpha)=\log_2\frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{\alpha^3}.
```
The ratio decreases from infinity to zero, giving a unique maximizer. It is $`\alpha_* =\lambda^{-2}\approx0.5698402910`$, because $`1-\alpha_*=\lambda^{-3}`$ follows from $`\lambda^3=\lambda+1`$. At that value,
```math
h_2(\alpha_*)=[2\alpha_*+3(1-\alpha_*)]\log_2\lambda
=(3-\alpha_*)\log_2\lambda.
```
The maximum rate is therefore $`\log_2\lambda`$.

## (f)
Any probability law supported on the admissible length-$`n`$ paths has entropy at most the logarithm of their count, so its asymptotic rate is at most $`\log_2\lambda`$. The maximizing Markov transition law just constructed achieves this combinatorial upper bound, explaining equality of the two answers.
